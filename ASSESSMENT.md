# Assessment: the 2025–26 maps, the reform proposals, and Neutral Lines

*Status as of October 26, 2026. Measured numbers come from this repository's pipeline. Every plan is scored with the same code and data, on 2020 Census blocks, against each state's districts in use for the November 2026 elections (the Census 120th-Congress block equivalency file). Partisan figures use the average of all 2016–2020 statewide races (VEST/ALARM); they describe how districts lean, not how elections will go.*

Every approach below is judged on three questions:

| | Question | What counts as evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **L: Legal** | Would it survive in court, federal and state? | Equal population ([*Wesberry*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/376/1/), [*Karcher*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/462/725/), [*Tennant*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/567/758/)), the Voting Rights Act after [*Louisiana v. Callais*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/608/24-109/) (2026), racial-gerrymandering doctrine ([*Shaw*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/509/630/), [*Alexander*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/602/22-807/)), each state's constitution and statutes, and pending litigation |
| **C: Consensus** | Could voters of both parties accept the *process*, even when they dislike the result? | Who draws; whether partisan intent is explicit; partisan symmetry and efficiency gap; transparency; polling on the method |
| **P: Practical** | Can election offices run it and courts review it, and will it last? | Split counties, municipalities and precincts; tiny split-precinct pieces; timing; how easily the next majority can undo it |

Ratings are **Strong / Mixed / Weak**, and each comes with its reasons, so readers can disagree with the weighting.

## 1. The 2025–26 mid-decade redraws

Nine states use new maps in November 2026 ([AP via CapRadio](https://www.capradio.org/articles/2026/09/28/9-states-have-new-us-house-districts-for-the-midterm-elections-after-trump-urged-changes/); [NCSL tracker](https://www.ncsl.org/redistricting-and-census/changing-the-maps-tracking-mid-decade-redistricting)): Alabama, California, Florida, Louisiana, North Carolina, Ohio, Tennessee, Texas and Utah. Redraw attempts failed or were blocked in Missouri (referendum pending), Virginia (amendment voided), Indiana (rejected by the state senate), and Maryland and New York (amendments pending for 2028).

Across these nine states, the 2026 maps give 77 Democratic-leaning seats against 87 under the formula. ### State by state

**Texas (enacted Aug. 29, 2025).**
- **What it is.** Drawn at the President's request to add Republican seats (designed R+5).
- **L: Mixed, now settled for 2026.** A three-judge court found it a racial gerrymander. The Supreme Court stayed that ruling and then summarily reversed it (Apr. 27, 2026). With partisan claims nonjusticiable in federal court ([*Rucho*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/588/18-422/)) and racial claims now harder after *Alexander* and *Callais*, its main legal exposure is gone.
- **C: Weak.** Openly partisan purpose, mid-decade timing, and a Democratic quorum break.
- **P: Mixed.** It triggered a counter-gerrymander in California.

**California (Prop 50, Nov. 4, 2025).**
- **What it is.** Voters set aside the citizen commission's map until 2030, explicitly as a counterweight to Texas (D+5).
- **L: Strong for 2026.** Upheld by a three-judge panel; the Supreme Court denied relief (Feb. 4, 2026).
- **C: Mixed.** It had a popular mandate (about 64%) but an openly partisan purpose, and it suspended the state's own independent commission.
- **P: Mixed.** It sunsets in 2030.

**North Carolina (Oct. 22, 2025).**
- **What it is.** A legislative redraw targeting NC-1. The governor has no veto over maps.
- **L: Mixed.** A federal panel allowed it, and state courts are closed to partisan claims since *Harper v. Hall* (2023).
- **C: Weak.** Partisan purpose.
- **P:** See the measurements above.

**Ohio (Oct. 31, 2025).**
- **What it is.** A bipartisan 7–0 commission vote (nominally 12–3 R).
- **L: Strong.** Bipartisan adoption under Art. XIX's own procedure, and it satisfies the county-split caps.
- **C: Mixed to strong.** A bipartisan vote, though the seat split favors one party.
- **P: Strong.** Zero counties split more than once.

**Utah (court-imposed, Nov. 10, 2025).**
- **What it is.** The court held the Legislature's repeal of voter-passed Prop 4 unconstitutional and imposed a plaintiffs' map with a Democratic-leaning Salt Lake seat.
- **L: Strong for 2026.** The state Supreme Court denied a stay.
- **C: Mixed.** It enforces a voter-passed standard, but the Legislature contests it.
- **P: Mixed.**

**Florida (May 4, 2026).**
- **What it is.** A legislative redraw (R+2 to R+4).
- **L: Weak to mixed.** Florida's Fair Districts amendment bars intent to favor a party. The map was sued the same day, a preliminary injunction was denied, and the appeal is pending.
- **C: Weak.**

**Alabama, Louisiana, Tennessee (after *Callais*, spring 2026).**
- **What they are.** Alabama restored its 2023 map, which drops the court-ordered second Black-opportunity district. Louisiana eliminated its second majority-Black district. Tennessee split Memphis.
- **L: Strong under federal law as of 2026.** *Callais* sharply limits Section 2 claims. State-law and intentional-discrimination claims remain: an Alabama court found intent as recently as May 2026.
- **C: Weak.** These maps reduce minority representation in states with racially polarized voting, which deeply divides public opinion.

**Missouri, Virginia, Indiana, Maryland, New York.**
- **Missouri.** Suspended pending a referendum (Prop A, Nov. 3, 2026). The process (referendum) is working as designed.
- **Virginia.** The amendment was voided on procedure, and the 2021 special-masters map stays.
- **Indiana.** The redraw was rejected by members of the majority party.
- **Maryland and New York.** These would remove or loosen state constraints for 2028. Both move away from neutral-process norms.

### Overall critique of the redraws

The 2025–26 maps are mostly *legal*. Federal courts have withdrawn from partisan claims, and *Callais* narrowed racial claims. But they fail the consensus test almost everywhere, by their authors' own description: each was drawn to add seats for one side, and each provoked a retaliatory redraw by the other. Their largest practical cost is instability. A map adopted by one majority mid-decade invites the next majority to redraw it.

## 2. Reform proposals

| Approach | L (legal) | C (consensus) | P (practical) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Independent citizen commissions** (AZ, CA, CO, MI) | Strong: [*AIRC*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/576/787/) (2015) upholds them for congressional maps | Strong: preferred over legislatures by nearly 2 to 1 even in California in Aug. 2025 ([poll](https://electionlawblog.org/?p=151568)) | Mixed: commissioners still exercise judgment, and California's was suspended by referendum in 2025 | Process trust depends on who selects commissioners. Outcomes vary. |
| **Iowa model** (nonpartisan staff draw with no political data; up-or-down legislative vote) | Strong | Strong in Iowa: 2021 plan passed 48–1 and 93–2 | Strong: whole counties, tiny deviations | Depends on a state culture of restraint. The legislature can reject plans, and did once in 2021. |
| **Court- or special-master-drawn maps** (PA 2018/2022, VA 2021, UT 2025) | Strong | Mixed: the losing side calls them judicial overreach | Strong | Remedial only, and they arrive late in the cycle. |
| **Ensemble / outlier testing** (MCMC, ReCom) | Strong as evidence; used in *Rucho*-era state cases and required in Utah | Mixed: technical, so trust depends on the experts | Strong as a *test*; it doesn't choose a map | Complements any drawing method; it is not one. |
| **Shortest-splitline and pure geometric algorithms** | Weak: straight lines cut cities and precincts, and they can't meet most state subdivision rules | Mixed: neutral, but alienating locally | Weak: many split precincts | The usual critique of "algorithmic maps". Neutral Lines was designed to answer it. |
| **Federal ban on mid-decade redistricting** (H.R. 4358, RESET Act, others) | Strong: within Congress's Art. I §4 power | Strong: it addresses the 2025–26 escalation directly and binds both parties equally | Strong | No bill has advanced. It stops the war but doesn't make the maps fairer. |
| **Federal partisan-gerrymandering standards** (Freedom to Vote Act versions) | Strong under Art. I §4 | Mixed: seen as partisan legislation | Mixed: enforcement through litigation | Stalled since 2022. |
| **Proportional representation** (Fair Representation Act: multi-member districts with ranked-choice voting) | Requires repealing 2 U.S.C. §2c | Mixed: a large change, unfamiliar to most voters | Mixed | It would remove gerrymandering's leverage altogether, but it is the largest departure from current practice. |
| **Removing state constraints** (Maryland Q3, Virginia 2026 amendment, New York) | Strong if passed | Weak: framed as retaliation | Mixed | Moves away from neutral process. |

## 3. Neutral Lines (this project)

### Legal

| Contiguous (shared edges, no corner-only links) | 50 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

| No bizarrely shaped districts | 37 | 0 | 13 | 0 |

| Split precincts | 33 | 0 | 17 | 0 |
| No tiny split-precinct pieces (ballot secrecy) | 14 | 14 | 22 | 0 |
| Iowa Code §42.4: each district within 1% of ideal | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Iowa Const. art. III §37: no county divided | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Ohio Art. XIX §2(B)(5) county limits | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Ohio §2(B)(4)(b): Cleveland and Cincinnati not split | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Ohio §2(B)(7): two districts share at most one county under 400,000 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Ohio §2(B)(8): each district includes a whole county | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| W. Va. Const. art. I §4: no county divided | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

Population: 19 of 50 states within one person; the largest gap is 225 people (Iowa). Counties split nationally: 372 (2026 maps: 408); municipalities: 815 (890); precincts: 1456 (1802).

- **Equal population.** Built from census blocks and equalized to the person where the keep-whole rule allows. Remaining gaps are a few people, from a consistently applied, neutral policy, far below the 0.79% the Supreme Court upheld in *Tennant*.
- **Contiguity.** Checked on the true union of each district's blocks, with corner-only contact counting as broken.
- **Race and the VRA.** Race is never an input, so there is no racial-gerrymandering exposure. After *Callais*, §2 requires a strong inference of intentional discrimination, which a rule fixed in law before the data arrive does not support. Where a court does find liability, the remedy can stay formulaic: lock the court-ordered district and rerun the formula on the rest.
- **State law.**
  - **Partisan data.** The partisan selection step is switched off where law bars partisan data (IA, MT, NE, NM, CA) or creates intent risk (FL).
  - **Ohio.** A state module enforces Ohio Const. art. XIX §2(B) in full: at least 65 counties whole, at most 18 split once and 5 split twice, Cleveland and Cincinnati unsplit, two districts sharing at most one county under 400,000, and each district's part of a county contiguous. The resulting plan passes every Ohio check, with a population gap of a few dozen people.
  - **Iowa and West Virginia.** Both constitutions forbid dividing counties in congressional districts (Iowa Const. art. III §37; W. Va. Const. art. I §4). State modules build whole-county plans that are as equal as whole counties allow, within Iowa's 1% statutory limit. West Virginia's plan is more equal than its enacted map; Iowa's enacted map is tighter.
  - **Precinct rules.** Colorado and New Mexico rules on precincts would need the same treatment.
- **Population base.** Raw Census counts. States that reallocate prisoners to their home addresses would run the same rule on their adjusted counts.

In California, Montana, Nebraska and New Mexico the symmetric choice and the most compact choice are the same plan, so their bans on partisan data cost nothing in fairness here.

### Consensus

**Strong by design.**
- The rule is fixed before the data and is blind to party, race and incumbents.
- It is deterministic and publishes a certificate that anyone can check.
- The fairness choice is symmetric and bounded: it can only pick among pre-certified compact plans.
- The Neutral Lines Compact pairs states so that neither party disarms alone.

**Limitations:**
- Compact, neutral maps still lean Republican where Democrats are concentrated in cities ([Chen & Rodden](https://doi.org/10.1561/100.00012033)). Democrats may see that as a cost, while Republicans may see the symmetry step as a thumb on the scale.
- Our answer is transparency: publish the whole candidate ensemble so that any lean is shown to come from geography rather than from a choice.

### Practical

**Mixed to strong.**
- **Strengths.** Every line follows a county, municipal, precinct or block boundary, and the plan is delivered as a standard block assignment file. County splits are close to the enacted maps nationally.
- **Weaker than the best human-drawn maps on some criteria.** A court special master in Connecticut, the Michigan commission and North Carolina's enacted map do better on town, precinct or compactness measures than the formula. The per-state checklist marks each of these **Review** rather than hiding it.

### Where Neutral Lines fits

Neutral Lines is not a replacement for the strongest existing processes. It is best used in three ways:
1. as the **required starting map** that commissions must justify departing from;
2. as the **default** when a state redraws mid-decade or misses a deadline;
3. as the **neutral benchmark** courts compare enacted maps against.

These are the roles where a rule fixed in advance, and checkable by anyone, adds the most consensus value.

## 4. Bottom line

| | Legal | Consensus | Practical |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2025–26 partisan redraws (TX, NC, FL, MO attempt; CA Prop 50 as counterweight) | Mostly legal | Weak | Unstable: invite retaliation |
| Post-*Callais* Southern redraws (AL, LA, TN) | Legal under federal law; state and intent claims remain | Weak | Stable until political control changes |
| Bipartisan or court processes (OH 2025, VA 2021, UT 2025, PA 2022) | Strong | Mixed to strong | Strong |
| Independent commissions; Iowa model | Strong | Strong | Strong, with human judgment |
| Neutral Lines | Strong: passes federal law and each state's own rules in all 50 states, with state modules for Iowa, West Virginia and Ohio | Strong by design | Strong as a default, starting map or benchmark |

The approaches most likely to build agreement share three traits: **the rules are fixed before anyone sees the results, both sides can verify them, and one side cannot change them alone.** Neutral Lines is built to have all three, but it still has to earn trust through independent review. The fastest consensus win available binds both parties equally: **a ban on mid-decade redistricting, with Neutral Lines as the default map for any state that breaks it.**
