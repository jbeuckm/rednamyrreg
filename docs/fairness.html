Fair from every side

Who gains, who gives something up, and is it legal?

A plan only lasts if people who dislike parts of it can still accept how it was made. This page looks at Neutral Lines from each group with a stake in congressional districts. For each one it sets out their legitimate concerns, what the formula offers them, and what it costs them, using numbers computed from the same data as the map. Below that are the legal status of every state's plan and an assessment of the 2025–26 redraws and the reform proposals.

Perspectives

The same plan, seen from each side

Fairness has no single definition. Each perspective below is held in good faith. What the formula can promise everyone is a neutral, verifiable process; it cannot promise every group the outcome it would prefer.

Legality

Legal status, state by state

Federal law requires equal population, single-member districts and compliance with the Voting Rights Act as narrowed by Louisiana v. Callais. Federal courts no longer hear partisan-gerrymandering claims, so state constitutions and statutes carry most of the remaining rules. The table applies the same automated checks to both plans and adds each state's own requirements where they bind.

Status key: ✓ Pass · ≈ Justified (allowed with a stated, neutral reason) · ! Review (worse than the enacted map or near a common flag) · ✕ Fail. Click a state to open it on the map.

Assessment

The 2025–26 maps and the reform proposals

Each approach is judged on three questions. Legal: would it survive in federal and state court? Consensus: could voters of both parties accept the process, even when they dislike the result? Practical: can election offices run it, and will it last?
